\begin{abstract}
Camouflaged object detection (COD) is deployed where cameras blur, add noise and compress, yet COD models are evaluated on clean images. Detectors built on the Segment Anything Model (SAM) now lead clean benchmarks, but their robustness has not been measured. We benchmark three specialist COD models, two SAM-based models and a zero-shot SAM probe under eight common corruptions at five severities on four datasets, with a held-out protocol that separates corruptions used in training from unseen ones. The SAM2-based model is the most accurate and the most robust; the largest model, SAM-Adapter, falls behind it under corruption. Noise, blur, JPEG and rain cause almost all of the damage, and models that read 1024-pixel inputs lose the most under them. We also train a lightweight adapter on a frozen MobileSAM encoder (\NParamsTrainable\,M trainable parameters) on a 4\,GB laptop GPU. On COD10K it outperforms all specialist models under corruption (mean \Sa{} \NOursCodMcorr{} vs.\ \NSinetCodMcorr{} for SINet-V2). Trained at 512 pixels it keeps the same accuracy under local corruptions, reaches the retention of SINet-V2 and runs in \NEffOursFiveLatency\,ms per image. Corruption training transfers modestly to unseen corruptions. Code, corruption seeds and per-image scores are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Camouflaged object detection (COD) segments objects that blend into their background~\cite{fan2020cod,fan2022sinetv2}. Its applications, such as wildlife monitoring with camera traps and drones, search and rescue, and visual inspection, rarely provide clean images: moving cameras blur, small sensors add noise, links compress, and weather degrades the scene. Camouflage cues are subtle, a faint boundary or a small break in texture, and these are exactly what such degradations remove.

Detectors built on the Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\cite{kirillov2023sam} now report the best clean COD results~\cite{chen2023samadapter,xiong2024sam2unet}. Their behaviour under degradation is unknown. The only COD corruption benchmark, COD10K-C~\cite{sayem2026cod10kc}, evaluates three specialist models on one dataset and includes no SAM-based model. Studies of SAM's robustness~\cite{huang2023robustsam_eval,qiao2023samcorruptions} use prompted, general-purpose segmentation, not camouflage.

This paper measures how SAM-based camouflage detectors behave under common corruptions and what robustness a student-scale GPU can buy. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A benchmark} of three specialist and two SAM-based COD models plus a zero-shot SAM probe, under the eight corruptions and five severities of COD10K-C on four test sets, with paired significance tests.
\item \textbf{A held-out-corruption protocol}: four corruptions may be used for training, four are only used for testing, so that robustness gains cannot come from memorising the augmentations.
\item \textbf{An analysis of input resolution}: models that read 1024-pixel inputs lose the most under local corruptions; training at 512 pixels removes this loss at a small clean-accuracy cost.
\item \textbf{A laptop-scale robust adapter}: a frozen MobileSAM encoder with \NParamsTrainable\,M trainable parameters, trained in \NTrainOurs\,h (\NTrainOursFive\,h at 512 pixels) on a 4\,GB GPU, that outperforms the specialist models under corruption on the two large test sets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Camouflaged object detection.} COD10K~\cite{fan2020cod} and SINet-V2~\cite{fan2022sinetv2} established the task and its standard protocol. Specialist models add task-specific modules: distraction mining in PFNet~\cite{mei2021pfnet}, mixed-scale processing in ZoomNet~\cite{pang2022zoomnet} and frequency decomposition in FEDER~\cite{he2023feder}. CAMO~\cite{le2019camo}, CHAMELEON~\cite{skurowski2018chameleon} and NC4K~\cite{lv2021nc4k} are the other standard test sets.

\textbf{SAM and adapters for COD.} SAM~\cite{kirillov2023sam} and SAM~2~\cite{ravi2024sam2} segment prompted objects but have no notion of camouflage. SAM-Adapter~\cite{chen2023samadapter} and its successors~\cite{chen2024sam2adapter,chen2025sam3adapter} insert trainable adapters~\cite{houlsby2019adapters,chen2022adaptformer} into a frozen SAM encoder; SAM2-UNet~\cite{xiong2024sam2unet} pairs a frozen SAM~2 encoder with a U-Net decoder; dual-stream adapters add depth~\cite{liu2025samdsa}. MobileSAM~\cite{zhang2023mobilesam} and EfficientSAM~\cite{xiong2024efficientsam} distil SAM into small encoders such as TinyViT~\cite{wu2022tinyvit}.

\textbf{Robustness to corruptions.} ImageNet-C~\cite{hendrycks2019imagenetc} introduced corruption benchmarks and the mean corruption error. SAM is robust to most corruptions except blur~\cite{huang2023robustsam_eval,qiao2023samcorruptions}; RobustSAM~\cite{chen2024robustsam} and GaRA-SAM~\cite{gara2025} make prompted SAM robust with consistency losses or gated low-rank adapters. For COD, KRNet~\cite{guan2025krnet} addresses low-quality inputs and COD10K-C~\cite{sayem2026cod10kc} benchmarks specialist models and proposes a lightweight robust model trained with corruption augmentation and a consistency loss. Augmentation with a consistency loss is also the idea behind AugMix~\cite{hendrycks2020augmix}. We use these ingredients deliberately and focus on what is new: SAM-family models, unseen corruptions, and the role of input resolution.

\section{Benchmark}
\textbf{Datasets.} We follow the standard protocol: training on COD10K-train and CAMO-train (\NImagesTrain{} images) and testing on CHAMELEON (\NImagesCham), CAMO (\NImagesCamo), COD10K (\NImagesCod) and NC4K (\NImagesNck). Mean image sizes range from \NSizeNckH$\times$\NSizeNckW{} (NC4K) to \NSizeChamH$\times$\NSizeChamW{} (CHAMELEON) pixels.

\textbf{Corruptions.} We use the eight corruptions and five severities of COD10K-C~\cite{sayem2026cod10kc}: Gaussian noise ($\sigma=8$--$40$), Gaussian blur (radius $0.8$--$2.5$), motion blur (horizontal kernel $3$--$11$), brightness and contrast (factor $0.85$--$0.30$), fog (blend with white, $\alpha=0.12$--$0.44$), JPEG (quality $70$--$20$) and rain. COD10K-C does not specify rain numerically; we synthesise streaks of increasing density and length, so rain results are not comparable with theirs. Corruptions are applied to the original image before each model's own resizing, with a seed derived from the dataset, image, corruption and severity, so every model sees identical corrupted images. We call noise, both blurs, JPEG and rain \emph{local} corruptions (they change pixels relative to their neighbours) and brightness, contrast and fog \emph{global} ones.

\textbf{Held-out protocol.} Gaussian noise, Gaussian blur, brightness and JPEG are \emph{seen}: they may be used for training. Motion blur, contrast, fog and rain are \emph{unseen}: each blur and illumination change has a related seen corruption, and weather is held out entirely.

\textbf{Metrics.} We report the structure measure \Sa~\cite{fan2017smeasure}, mean E-measure $E_\phi$~\cite{fan2018emeasure}, weighted F-measure $F_\beta^w$~\cite{margolin2014wfm}, MAE and Dice, computed per image with PySODMetrics~\cite{pysodmetrics}. Robustness is summarised by mCorr, the mean score over the 40 corrupted conditions; retention, mCorr divided by the clean score; and mCE, the corruption error relative to SINet-V2~\cite{hendrycks2019imagenetc}. Differences between models are tested with a paired bootstrap over per-image scores (5{,}000 resamples, 95\% confidence intervals).

\textbf{Models.} We evaluate SINet-V2~\cite{fan2022sinetv2}, PFNet~\cite{mei2021pfnet}, ZoomNet~\cite{pang2022zoomnet}, SAM2-UNet~\cite{xiong2024sam2unet} and SAM-Adapter (ViT-H)~\cite{chen2023samadapter} from their released weights, following each official test script, including its input size. FEDER~\cite{he2023feder} is scored from its released prediction maps on clean images only. SAM2-Adapter and SAM3-Adapter released no COD weights. As an analysis probe, MobileSAM~\cite{zhang2023mobilesam} prompted with the ground-truth box shows how SAM delineates an object whose location is known; it is not a competitor. All reproduced clean \Sa{} values are within \NReproMaxDelta{} of the published ones.

\section{Lightweight Robust Adapter}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig_architecture.pdf}
\caption{Our model. Only the coloured parts are trained (\NParamsTrainable\,M of \NParamsTotal\,M parameters). The MobileSAM encoder is frozen; zero-initialised adapters in its ten transformer blocks, four learned prompt tokens and the SAM mask decoder are trained.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure*}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows our model. \textbf{Encoder.} We freeze the TinyViT encoder of MobileSAM~\cite{zhang2023mobilesam,wu2022tinyvit}. A bottleneck adapter runs in parallel with the MLP of each of its ten transformer blocks~\cite{chen2022adaptformer}: $x \leftarrow x + \mathrm{MLP}(x) + W_\mathrm{up}\,\mathrm{GELU}(W_\mathrm{down}\,\mathrm{LN}(x))$, with a hidden width of a quarter of the block width. $W_\mathrm{up}$ starts at zero, so training begins from exactly the pretrained encoder.
\textbf{Prompt-free decoding.} Instead of user clicks, the SAM mask decoder receives four learned prompt tokens and a learned dense prompt; two tokens are initialised as SAM's embedding of a box around the whole image. The decoder is trained; its upsampled first mask logit gives the camouflage map.

\textbf{Training.} The supervised loss is the weighted BCE plus weighted IoU structure loss~\cite{fan2020pranet}. Each training image gives a clean view and a view corrupted with a random \emph{seen} corruption at a random severity, under the same crop and flip. The loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mathcal{L}_s(\hat{y}) + \mathcal{L}_s(\hat{y}')\big) + \lambda \,\big\| \sigma(\hat{y}') - \mathrm{sg}[\sigma(\hat{y})] \big\|_2^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{y}$ and $\hat{y}'$ are the clean and corrupted logits, $\mathrm{sg}$ stops gradients, so the clean prediction acts as a fixed target, and $\lambda=1$. We use AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} (learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, cosine schedule), an effective batch of 8, fp16 and gradient checkpointing, for 30 epochs. \emph{Ours-Clean} uses neither corruption nor consistency; \emph{Ours-Aug} corrupts half of the images without consistency. We train the full model at 1024 pixels, \ours{} (1024), and at 512 pixels, \ours{} (512), the latter with three seeds. Training takes \NTrainOurs\,h and \NTrainOursFive\,h on one RTX 3050 Laptop GPU with 4\,GB.

\textbf{Input resolution} (Fig.~\ref{fig:resolution}, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The two models that read 1024-pixel inputs keep the least of their score under local corruptions: \NSamadCodRetLocal{} for SAM-Adapter and \NOursCodRetLocal{} for \ours{} (1024), against \NSinetCodRetLocal{} for SINet-V2 at 352 pixels. Shrinking a $\approx$\NSizeCodH$\times$\NSizeCodW{} image to 352 pixels averages neighbouring pixels, which weakens noise, JPEG blocks and rain streaks and makes blur smaller, so the same corruption is effectively weaker for a low-resolution model. Shrinking the input of the trained 1024-pixel model only at test time lowers its accuracy everywhere (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}b). Training at 512 pixels instead leaves its accuracy under local corruptions unchanged (\NSigOursFiveOursCodLocalDiff, [\NSigOursFiveOursCodLocalLo, \NSigOursFiveOursCodLocalHi]), costs \NOursCodClean{}$\to$\NOursFiveCodClean{} on clean COD10K, and raises retention to \NOursFiveCodRet, the level of SINet-V2. On NC4K and CAMO, whose images are smaller, the 512-pixel model is better than the 1024-pixel one also on clean images (Table~\ref{tab:clean}). The useful input size tracks the native image resolution; larger inputs mainly expose the model to corruption.

\section{Limitations}
Our corruptions are synthetic and applied one at a time; real degradations combine and vary across the image, and our rain is our own design. The large models were evaluated but not retrained with corruption augmentation, so robustness training is tested only on our adapter. Two unseen corruptions, contrast and fog, barely affect any model, which limits what the unseen split can show. All timings come from one laptop GPU. Input resolution and architecture differ across the baselines, so our resolution analysis is controlled only for our own model.

\section{Conclusion}
SAM-based camouflage detectors are not inherently fragile: SAM2-UNet is both the most accurate and the most robust model we tested. Size alone does not buy robustness, and local corruptions, especially rain, blur and noise, do almost all the damage. A model's input resolution decides how strongly these corruptions reach it. A frozen MobileSAM with \NParamsTrainable\,M trainable parameters, trained on a 4\,GB laptop GPU with corruption augmentation, outperforms the specialist models under corruption on the large test sets, and at 512 pixels it matches their robustness at lower cost. Future work includes resolution-aware training, multi-scale decoder features~\cite{ke2023hqsam} and real-world degraded test data.
